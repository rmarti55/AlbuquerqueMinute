SUMMER CAMP

I re-joined forces with my friends at Hilltown Commons to host a weekend of good old fun. If you want to recreate some childhood memories (perhaps this time with aching joints) you’re in luck my friend. Pack your bags, we’re going to SUMMER CAMP.

The weekend will be wild, and it will be fun. Here’s the breakdown:

There’ll be four teams competing for points across various activities from archery to tug of war and javelin throwing, just to name a few.

We’ll stroll through the woods, identify trees, craft tinctures, and gather around the fire. We even have a professional storyteller to share spooky local lore — and unlike when we were kids, this summer camp has a pub.

In the end, only one team will have enough points to claim victory and eternal bragging rights, but we’ll all be winners.

Whether you wish to stay for the weekend and soak it all in or just come for the main event, you can find all the details in that little link below.

SULPHURS

I met up with Sean — our fly fishing guide — on the Delaware River around noon. The sky was dark and the air quality was bad (being outside was not advised).

The fish on this river are big, they are strong, and at this time of year they are exceptionally picky.

The sulphur mayfly hatch was in full swing. Up to now, these bugs have spent their lives crawling around on the river bottom. When the time is right, they rise to the surface, breaking from their exoskeletons and taking flight to live a brief moment above the water. Their goals are simple: to mate, lay eggs on the river surface, and continue the cycle of life. 

We boarded our drift boat, headed down river, and tied a sulphur pattern onto the end of our line. In fly fishing, it’s “matching the hatch”, ideally with a very good imitation of the real bugs.

As the day went on, we moved through various sizes of flies — even tying on some larger patterns to see if we could find some luck. Alas, no fish.

By 3PM we spotted a large brown trout consistently feeding on the surface. Standing at the bow, I was determined: ”Cast, feed the line, reset, cast again.”

After 90 minutes, she finally investigated our compilation of feather and fur that made up the pseudo-sulphur. Slowly, she bumped the fly with her nose as it drifted over… and dismissed it. We continued floating down river. 

Shortly after, we had some luck and got two fish to bite, but both instantly snapped off the 6x tippet (very thin line). There was victory in those moments of defeat.

Around us, merganser moms and babies floated by, beavers of unusual size slapped their tails, and eagles soared. Despite more rising fish and more fly changes, we were met with nothing but refusals. This is sulphur season on the Delaware. 

9:30PM. There was no moon, not a star in the sky, but we sat anchored near the takeout. My determination — married with some joyful frustration — kept me casting into the darkness. Over and over I fed the slack far downstream and kept light tension on the line. At long last, I had a fish on. An 18 inch brown trout.

We thanked the fish and the noble Delaware, and made the long drive home.
