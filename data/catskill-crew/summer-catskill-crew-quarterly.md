DO YOU KNOW WHO THIS IS?

This year, we’ve seen an outbreak of a specific native caterpillar across parts of the southern Catskills.

They have a ravenous appetite and have stripped swaths of mountainside trees bare of their leaves.

To be clear, this outbreak is cyclical — these populations surge every 10 to 20 years — and while the trees don’t look happy or healthy, most will push out a new set of leaves later in the season.

Now, take a look at this little fella and try to guess the name of the species that is causing all the trouble.

THE LIFE OF

A DEAD RED OAK

CONTRIBUTED BY IVY MACKEY

A Red Oak (Quercus rubra) fell in my backyard last December. The first bug I discovered in this fallen tree was a Woolly Bear (Pyrrharctia isabella) in early spring when I was considering rolling it to a more aesthetic location in my yard. When I accidentally uncovered Woolly Bear in their astounding hibernation, I quickly tucked them back in to complete their slumber and decided the log was already too occupied to be relocated. A whole world of tiny beings has begun moving in! Now it is July and I crouch beside the dead tree. The softened heartwood, fungal consumption, and thousands of tunnels carved throughout are signaling the next life of this forest giant.

I observe beautiful swirling geometric patterns now visible beneath the sloughed off outer bark. These intricate tunnels were created by Minute Oak Bark Beetles (Pseudopityophthorus minutissimus). The adults likely chewed their way into the tree while it was still standing but so aged or stressed that the tree’s defenses had been weakened. This allowed the beetles to lay eggs under the bark and the larvae that hatched chewed their own tunnels back out.

Ants of multiple species march over my feet, using blades of grass as highways leading in and out of the tree’s tiny passageways. Red (Camponotus chromaiodes) and black (Camponotus pennsylvanicus) carpenter ants do not eat the tree, but chew the now softened wood into pathways leading to their various chambers. Carpenter ants clear the way for secondary move-ins. The much smaller Bicolored Pennant Ant (Tetramorium bicarinatum) will utilize abandoned carpenter ant galleries to begin their own colonies within the heartwood of the oak.

A Pure Green Sweatbee (Augochlora pura) peeks their head out at me with astounding curiosity. These metallic green gems use old beetle tunnels as starting points, then excavate further to create burrows and brood cells where females will lay their eggs. Mated females will hibernate deep within the tree through the winter and emerge as one of the earliest spring pollinators.

Greenhouse Millipedes (Oxidus gracilis) sit half emerged from their burrow openings in graceful S-shapes. They act as beneficial detritivores, meaning they feed on the decaying wood. They are converting rot into nutrient-rich humus that will in turn feed the surrounding ecosystem.

As tiny as they are, these bugs are essential components in the life cycle of the forest: transmuting death and decay into a whole new world. It feels both humbling and reassuring that a living Red Oak, as magnificent and hardy as it is, will one day be slowly consumed by the tiniest mouths, made into the tiniest apartments, and become a nourishing feast that feeds the forest floor and mycelial web that raised that tree.
