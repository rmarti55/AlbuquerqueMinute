MAPLE SYRUP

When winter loosens its grip on the Catskills, maple syrup season arrives. That magical freeze-thaw cycle kicks in — warm days wake the trees, cold nights keep the sap sweet, and suddenly the maples are ready to share their liquid gold.

Getting sap is surprisingly straightforward. Find a healthy sugar maple (at least 10-12 inches in diameter), drill about 2 inches into the south-facing side, tap in a spile, and hang your bucket. The real work comes next. It takes roughly 40 gallons of that watery sap to make just one gallon of syrup, boiled down through a process that demands patience, precision, and plenty of firewood. A single tap might yield enough sap for about a quart of finished syrup over the season — not much reward for the effort, which explains why the good stuff commands its price.

If you're curious to see the process firsthand or just want to stock up on some fresh Catskill syrup, check out the events section below for maple celebrations happening throughout the region this weekend. There's nothing quite like the steam rising from a sugar shack to signal that spring is on its way.

SKY REPORT

Mercury reaches its greatest eastern elongation on March 8th, giving us a rare peek at this sneaky little fella. You can catch it hovering about 17° above the western horizon just after sunset, where it'll hang out for roughly 90 minutes before disappearing at 7:28 PM. Since it orbits so close to the Sun, Mercury usually stays hidden in the glare, but for about a week around the 8th, we will have a few good shots. Find yourself a spot with a clear view west and soak it in.

Last Week: What famous fly pattern did Theodore Gordon adapt from English patterns to better match Catskill insect hatches, revolutionizing American dry fly fishing? The answer was The Quill Gordon, which is pretty much Catskill fishing royalty. It perfectly mimics the Epeorus Pleuralis mayfly. Designed by Theodore Gordon himself back in the late 1800s, this beauty was one of America's first true dry flies and remains a staple in every self-respecting angler's box around these parts.
